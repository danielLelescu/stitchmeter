Stitchmeter

Compact, frictionless, 100%-mechanical force measurement system to measure knot tightening force (Phase I) and tensile resistance (Phase II) as metrics for suture quality, to train surgeons in disaster zones.

Project at a glance

2 PHASES - 3 THREADS

Design challenge

INTERNAL KNOT FORCE AND TENSILE RESISTANCE

Internal knot force goes up to 8N, while knot tensile resistance goes up to 96N. Input force must not exceed 10N during resistance testing.

Measurement graduations must be at least 0.5mm, distinguishable to the naked eye.

The device must resist at least 100,000 cycles, and be operable in extreme environments (e.g sand, snow).

The device must have an estimated production cost less than 300CHF.

Technical approach

HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS

Flexure realisation

Translation from Ideal to Flexure

Ideal - Viewing the system with ideal components makes its parts much clearer. We observe 3 clearly defined blocks: A, B, and C.

Phase I: Block B is mobile (1 DOF) while Block A is immobilized (by a ridge not shown beneath the blocks). As Block B is pulled towards Block A by the tightening of a suture thread, its displacement is amplified by the lever effect on the linked green needle (far right). Block B's displacement is translated into force by the K1 spring and displayed on the first ruler in yellow. Block B's rigidity can be discretely modified by the "lames à flamber" (buckling blades) which make lateral movement more challenging if toggled into a buckled state by the camshaft in blue, operated via knob.

Phase II: Block B is immobilized while Block A (attached to Block B via knot) is pulled by Block C (1 DOF), which is in turn pulled by the lever in blue. The distance between Blocks A and C is translated into force by the K2 spring, and displayed on the second ruler in yellow.

Flexure - Linear bearings are replaced with pairs of blades (1 DOF), linkages are replaced with single blades (2 DOF), and pivots are replaced with crossed blades (1 DOF).

Block B is guided by a rigid pair of blades which emulate Spring K1. Block C is implemented with a "4-blade table" design as proposed by our professor, which allows for discrete rigidity modification (and a non-linear effect on the rigidity profile) by nature of transversal spring which pre-stresses the blades.

Translation to flexure mechanisms entails a delicate balance between rigidity, blade resistance, parasitic motion, and weight. My team and I utilized Python scripts and AI to find suitable sets of dimensions optimized for each criterion.

Please see my team's report for further detail on how we designed and implemented the system.

Key results

WHAT WORKED / WHAT CHANGED

My Initial Pitch

For this project, much emphasis was placed on the design process. 5 design boards, each with a distinct solution proposal, were requested as our first deliverable. We would then select the design board which we believed to have the most potential, and develop it into our final product.

On this board, we see the beginnings of Block A (on the left here) and B (on the right here), switching from mobile to immobile or vice versa between Phases I and II. The separate-ruler approach and rigid pairs of blades also remained all the way through the design process. One major difference is a complete lack of thread-changing, instead I opted for having three near-identical "modules" side-by-side, with differing rigidities for different threads. Block C was the subject of much debate, here a similar concept appears in orange underneath my 3-stage lever, designed for maximum amplification. I also attempted to prototype a rigidity step function for Phase II, with staged springs.
